Jalandhar is where our studio is based, and it is also one of Punjab's busiest wedding markets — marriage palaces strung along NH-44 and the Phagwara highway, farmhouses off the Kapurthala road, hotel banquets near the Cantt, and a steady run of home weddings in Model Town, Urban Estate and the older city neighbourhoods. None of that is unusual for a Punjabi wedding season. What's easy to underestimate, if you haven't planned a Jalandhar wedding before, is how much the venue and the season actually change the makeup plan — not the look itself, but the timeline and the base technique behind it.

## Marriage-hall light versus home light

A big share of Jalandhar weddings happen in one of two very different settings, and each one needs a different approach:

- **Marriage palaces and banquet halls** on the Phagwara highway, Nakodar Road and around the Cantt tend to run warm, dim, decorative lighting built for the evening function, not for close-up photography. An HD or airbrush base with stronger definition around the eyes and contour holds up far better here than a light, barely-there look that can go flat under a function hall's lighting rig and in flash photography.
- **Getting ready at home** — still the norm for a large number of Jalandhar brides — usually has better daylight for building the base, but less room to work in and more family members moving through the space. We plan the artist team and the order of hair, makeup and draping differently for a home setup than for a banquet suite, so the bride isn't rushed when the photographer, the sisters and the choora ceremony are all happening around her at once.

## Winter fog changes the morning, not the makeup

Jalandhar's wedding season runs heavy through the cooler months, and from late November through January, morning fog on NH-44 and the Grand Trunk Road is routine enough that we plan for it as a default, not an exception. This matters more than people expect, because a Punjabi wedding morning is already a tightly sequenced relay: maiyan or vatna the day before, then hair and makeup, then jewellery and chooda, then the family photos, then the baraat or Anand Karaj at a fixed muhurat time.

Fog doesn't change how we build the face, but it changes when we start. If the band, the decorators, outstation relatives or even the bride's own going-away car are coming in from outside the city, a foggy morning can push everyone's arrival back by 30 to 45 minutes without warning. We build that buffer into the timeline up front — starting the base earlier rather than promising a tight finish — so the bride is ready well before the muhurat instead of being touched up in a rush while the family is already waiting at the gurdwara or the mandap. ## Planning the actual day

For a typical Jalandhar wedding day, the sequence we work to looks like this:

1. **Skin prep and base** start first, timed to the venue's light — earlier and with a slightly warmer base for a dim banquet hall, a touch cooler and lighter for a bright home setup.
2. **Eyes and the rest of the face** are built next, matched to the outfit and the ceremony — a softer daytime look for a sagan or roka function, a stronger, photograph-ready look for the main wedding and reception.
3. **Chooda, kaleere, jewellery and draping** go last and are timed to stay fresh for photographs rather than sitting through an hour of waiting before the baraat arrives.
4. **A fog or delay buffer** is built into the start time itself during the November–January season, rather than left to chance.

## The honest bottom line

A Jalandhar wedding doesn't need a different makeup look so much as a timeline that respects the venue's actual lighting and the season's actual weather.
